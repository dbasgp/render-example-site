Drainage·6 min read·Updated Oct 2025

Gravity, pump, or both — drainage for ceiling units.

A 68 L/day unit drops a small bucket of water every day. Every day. If that water does not have an obvious, low-friction path out of the ceiling, you will eventually find it staining the plasterboard below.

Three drainage situations

Almost every ceiling dehumidifier install falls into one of three patterns.

  • Gravity to a nearby drain. The unit sits above a bathroom, kitchen, or balcony where you can drop a line straight down and connect to an existing floor trap or condensate stack. This is the simplest and most reliable option. Use it whenever you can.
  • Pump up to reach a drain. The unit is above a living room or bedroom with no nearby outlet. The internal condensate pump lifts water up and across to a destination — usually an FCU drain in the same plenum, or out to a balcony.
  • Pump then gravity. The most common commercial layout. Pump lifts above the ceiling, then a continuous downward run carries water by gravity to the building's drainage stack.

How much head you actually have

DBA's UTC range has an integrated condensate pump rated for 1.8 m of head. GEC68 and GEC145 share the same pump. Larger commercial GEC and GE units offer 5 m to 10 m of head. The number on the spec sheet, though, is the total of:

  • Vertical lift from the pump outlet to the highest point of the line
  • Friction loss in the horizontal run (roughly 0.05 m of head per metre of 16 mm hose at typical flow)
  • Bend losses (each 90° elbow ≈ 0.1 m of head)

For a UTC68 (1.8 m head), a 1 m vertical lift plus 8 m of horizontal hose plus three bends = 1.0 + 0.4 + 0.3 = 1.7 m. You are right at the limit. We would shorten the run or add a relay tank if we could.

The continuous-fall rule

Once water is above the ceiling void and on a horizontal run, gravity does the work. The rule is a continuous fall of at least 1:50 — 20 mm per metre — toward the destination. Any uphill section creates a trap that holds water, slows the pump, and eventually grows a biofilm that closes the line.

Sounds obvious. The most common reason we are called in to investigate a "leaking dehumidifier" is exactly this: a drain line that left the unit at 1.8 m, ran 4 m horizontally with one sag in the middle, and slowly built a biofilm plug.

Check valves and air gaps

Any lifted drain line should have a check valve. Without it, every time the pump cycles off, water in the lifted section runs back down and partially refills the drain pan. The pump runs again. Repeat. The result is constant cycling, wasted pump life, and excess noise.

Where the drain line connects to a building sanitary stack, an air gap or trap is needed by code in most jurisdictions, including Singapore PUB requirements for commercial sites. Skip it and sewer gases find their way into your ceiling. Cheap, easy, mandatory.

Insulating the drain line

Singapore plenums sit at 28–35°C. The drain line carries cool condensate. The line itself will sweat — not enough to drip, but enough over months to discolour adjacent insulation and timber. For commercial installs and any residential install where the drain runs above a finished ceiling, we wrap the first 1.5 m of line in closed-cell foam.

What to actually check after commissioning

Before you sign off, do this:

  1. Run the unit in dehumidify mode for 30 minutes
  2. Walk to the drain destination and confirm water is arriving
  3. Pour 500 ml of water directly into the unit's drain pan via the test port (most DBA units have one). Confirm it discharges within 60 seconds.
  4. Switch the unit off and listen for water trickling back through the line — if you hear it, the check valve is wrong or missing
  5. Open the ceiling access panel after 24 hours and look. Dry void = good. Any damp = stop and re-route.

Sanity test

Drain head limit ≥ (vertical lift) + (horizontal length × 0.05) + (number of 90° bends × 0.1), with at least 20% margin. If your numbers do not leave 20% margin, change the route, not the unit.

When to specify an external pump

For high lifts (3 m+) or very long horizontal runs (15 m+), the integrated pump on a UTC or GEC68 will not be enough. The two practical solutions are:

  • Move up to a GEC145 or GE-series unit with a higher-head internal pump
  • Use a small external condensate pump tank under the unit (Aspen Mini, Sauermann SI-30, or equivalent) and let it do the lift

The external tank route is the standard for commercial installs in Singapore where ceiling voids are deep and stacks are far apart. It costs a few hundred dollars more on install and saves the project from a difficult retrofit later.
